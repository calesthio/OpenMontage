You may never have heard of it, but this ten‑day mission in March 1969 was the first flight of the full Apollo spacecraft — command module, service module, and lunar module flying together as one. | B2 | Three men flew Apollo 9. Commander James A. McDivitt, a Gemini 4 veteran and commander of Apollo 9, first flight of the complete set of Apollo hardware and first flight of the Lunar Module. Command Module Pilot David R. Scott, who flew Gemini 8 and Apollo 9, later commanded Apollo 15, and logged 546 hours and 54 minutes in space including 20 hours 46 minutes of EVA; he later became Director of NASA’s Dryden Flight Research Center. Lunar Module Pilot Russell L. Schweickart, from NASA’s third astronaut group in October 1963, logged 241 hours in space on Apollo 9 — the first manned flight of the Lunar Module — and during EVA tested the portable life support system backpack subsequently used on lunar surface explorations. Their backup crew: Charles Conrad Jr. as commander, Alan L. Bean as Lunar Module Pilot, Richard F. Gordon Jr. as Command Module Pilot. The two spacecraft had names: the Lunar Module, nicknamed Spider (LM‑3), and the Command and Service Module, nicknamed Gumdrop (CSM‑104). This was the first manned flight of the LM — the first manned flight of all lunar hardware in Earth orbit. | A9-005, A9-006, A9-007, A9-008, A9-009, A9-010 | |
| B3 | Two hours and 41 minutes after launch, CSM Gumdrop turned to face Spider, still attached to the S‑IVB third stage, and completed the first successful docking of the Apollo program. About an hour later, springs ejected the docked spacecraft from the S‑IVB. The crew pressurized the tunnel between Gumdrop and Spider, removed the CM hatch, connected umbilicals to power the LM while the two spacecraft remained docked, then replaced the hatch. | A9-018, A9-018, A9-017 | |
| B4 | On flight day 3, McDivitt and Schweickart put on spacesuits and transferred to the LM through the tunnel connection to perform a systems checkout. This included a 367‑second firing of the LM descent engine to simulate the throttle pattern to be used during a lunar landing mission — McDivitt controlled the final 59 seconds, varying the thrust from 10 to 40 percent and shutting it off manually — the first crewed throttling of an engine in space. They also tested the landing and rendezvous radars and made a 5‑minute television broadcast from inside Spider. | A9-011, A9-011, A9-011 | |
| B5 | The flight plan called for a two‑hour EVA to simulate external transfer rescue techniques — the only planned EVA from the LM before an actual lunar landing. But Schweickart suffered space motion sickness, vomiting twice during the first two days, so the EVA was delayed and then shortened from a planned 2 hours to 37.5 minutes. Instead, he climbed out of the LM porch for a 37.5‑minute EVA, testing the EVA mobility unit including the portable life support system backpack — this was the first and only EVA before the lunar landing and the only opportunity to test the PLSS in space. Schweickart floated out the LM side hatch onto the front porch using gold‑painted foot restraints ("golden slippers"); Scott opened the CM side hatch and floated partway out (stand‑up EVA); both spent about 37 minutes outside. Schweickart’s only connection to the LM was a 25‑foot nylon rope; he maneuvered on handrails near the hatch (found them easy), took photographs, and described rain squalls over KSC. Scott performed a stand‑up EVA (about 1 hour 4 minutes) from the CM hatch without a PLSS, connected to CM life support via umbilicals and a Pressure Control Valve, and filmed Schweickart. Scott stood in the open side hatch of Gumdrop observing Schweickart’s EVA activities. Mission Control called it a complete success — the first and only spacesuit/PLSS test before the Moon landing. | A9-004, A9-025, A9-026, A9-027, A9-028, A9-029, A9-030, A9-027 | |
| B6 | Flight day 5: McDivitt and Schweickart undocked in Spider for a 6 hour 23‑minute independent flight while Scott remained in Gumdrop. About 45 minutes after undocking, Spider’s DPS engine fired 19 seconds (first at 10% thrust then throttled to 40%) beginning separation and placing it about 50 miles from Gumdrop; a 22‑second DPS burn opened the distance to about 100 miles. To begin rendezvous Spider fired ascent‑stage RCS thrusters for 32 seconds while jettisoning the Descent Stage (which remained in orbit until March 22, burning up over the Indian Ocean); first APS burn lasted 3 seconds, second APS burn 38 seconds put Spider on an intercept course with Gumdrop; two small course corrections refined the trajectory. Spider stopped about 100 feet from Gumdrop for a pitchover maneuver; Scott photographed the ascent stage including its engine ("the biggest, friendliest, funniest looking Spider I've ever seen"); McDivitt brought Spider in for smooth docking; the craft flew separately for 6 hours 23 minutes. Two hours after docking McDivitt and Schweickart rejoined Scott in Gumdrop and shortly after jettisoned Spider; Mission Control commanded Spider's APS to fire for six minutes into a highly elliptical Earth orbit from which it did not decay until October 23 1981. | A9-020, A9-021, A9-022, A9-023, A9-024 | |
| B7 | Apollo 9 was the first manned flight of the LM; first manned flight of all lunar hardware in Earth orbit. The mission demonstrated docked and undocked maneuvers, performed a lunar module–command module rendezvous, and demonstrated extra‑vehicular capability — everything Apollo 11 would need to land on the Moon. The backpack Schweickart tested in space was subsequently used on the lunar surface explorations. Every system that would later carry humans to the Moon had been flown, tested, and proven in Earth orbit. | A9-010, A9-004, A9-019, A9-026, A9-010 | |
