## 2. Reused / inauthentic content (MEASURED: policy text, not channel data)

YouTube renamed "repetitious content" → **"inauthentic content"** effective **July 15, 2025**. It reviews channels for content that is "repetitive or mass-produced." The related **reused content** policy covers channels that repurpose content from YouTube or another online source "without adding significant original commentary, substantive modifications, or educational or entertainment value."

Key rules (paraphrased from support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392):
- Content must be original and "authentic"; if you borrow from others, you must change it significantly.
- Not mass-produced, generic, repetitive, or manipulative; made for enjoyment/education, not solely for views.
- **Applies channel-wide.** If reviewers can't tell you made the content, monetization may be removed from the **entire channel**.
- Reused content IS allowed to monetize IF viewers can tell there's a "meaningful difference" between the original and your video (e.g., commentary, narrative, substantive editing). But copyright still applies separately.
- Reused content is **separate from Copyright enforcement** — no copyright claim/strike is needed for a reused-content violation; permission/fair use do not cure it.

FAQ (support.google.com/youtube/community-guide/271248162): AI use (images, voiceover, script, entire video) does not automatically cause suspension, but the channel must still be clearly original; AI mass-produced content is at risk.

## 3. AI / synthetic & altered content disclosure

From support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491 (Disclosing use of GenAI content):
- **Must disclose** GenAI content that is realistic and: makes a real person say/do something they didn't; alters footage of a real event or place; generates a realistic scene that didn't occur; or creates music as the main focus.
- **AI voice narration:** realistic synthetic voice used to narrate = must disclose via the "AI use" setting in YouTube Studio.
- **Don't have to disclose:** non-realistic content, minor edits, production assistance (script ideas, captions), **cloning your own voice** for voiceovers/dubs.
- Non-disclosure risks manual labeling, content removal, or YPP suspension. Disclosure does **not** limit audience or monetization eligibility.
- YouTube may auto-apply labels (GenAI tools, C2PA metadata, detection systems).

## 4. Advertiser-friendly guidelines (space/aviation archival relevance)

From http://support.google.com/youtube/answer/6162278:
- Videos not suitable for ads include: inappropriate language, violence, adult content, shocking content, harmful acts, hateful content, firearms, controversial issues, sensitive events, tobacco.
- **Violence/shocking content:** "Content where the focal point is on blood, violence, or injury, when presented without other context, is not suitable for advertising." Space crashes, test-flight accidents, and combat aviation footage can fall here.
- **EDSA context exception:** Exceptions can be made when content has clear **educational, documentary, scientific, or artistic (EDSA)** context. A crash shown in a documentary/explanatory context is more likely ad-suitable than crash footage presented for shock.
- 2026 update: controversial-issue content became eligible for ads when non-graphic and dramatized (Jan 2026); brand safety increasingly set by advertiser inventory controls, not just the platform.

## 5. Content ID on NASA media

NASA works of the U.S. federal government are **public domain** (no copyright). However, YouTube's Content ID system allows third parties (broadcasters, news orgs) to upload the same public-domain footage and **claim** re-uploads. Documented cases:
- Ars Technica (Aug 2012): five media organizations claimed one creator's NASA Curiosity-rover video.
- Trust and Safety Foundation (2021, re 2012): Scripps broadcasts caused NASA's own public-domain Mars landing footage to be taken down; "NASA reps are used to having to contact YouTube to get their own public domain uploads restored."
- Ars Technica (Jun 2020): National Geographic briefly claimed NASA/SpaceX Demo-2 footage.
- Techdirt (2009): "only public domain if it's the original NASA video. Everyone else's use of and additions to it are NOT in the public domain."

Result: a channel re-uploading NASA footage may receive Content ID claims (ads diverted to claimant, video blocked/tracked) even though the footage is public domain. Claims can be disputed but are slow.

## 6. Concrete do / don't rules for our channel

| # | Do | Don't |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Add **significant original commentary, narrative, and substantive editing** to archival footage so viewers see a "meaningful difference." | Re-upload raw NASA/USAF footage with minimal changes (reused-content / inauthentic-content risk, channel-wide). |
| 2 | Use your **own voice** for narration (no AI disclosure required). | Use a realistic third-party AI voice without setting the "AI use" disclosure. |
| 3 | If using any realistic AI imagery/voice/footage, set **AI use = Yes** in YouTube Studio at upload. | Omit AI disclosure on realistic synthetic content. |
| 4 | Frame crashes/test failures in **documentary/educational context** (EDSA) to support ad suitability. | Present crash/blood/injury footage as the focal point for shock value (likely limited/no ads). |
| 5 | Credit NASA as source; avoid NASA logos/insignia in promotional use (14 CFR 1221). | Imply NASA endorsement of your channel or use NASA insignia as cover art/merch. |
| 6 | Expect **Content ID claims** on public-domain NASA footage; keep records proving public-domain status; be ready to dispute. | Assume public domain = no claims; rely on ad revenue from a single archival-heavy video. |
